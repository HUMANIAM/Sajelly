# Writing for people first

> *Raise your words, not your voice. It is rain that grows flowers, not thunder.*  
> — Jalal al-Din Rumi

Everything we write in engineering — code and comments, docs and reports, config, commit messages, even the labels on our data — is read by a person in the end. Often a tired one, scanning for a single answer. Write for that person. Not for the machine, and not for show.

Six habits carry most of the weight.

**Be understood before anything else.** Prefer the plain word to the clever one, and the short sentence to the long. Use the simplest language the idea allows. When a term can’t be avoided, define it once, in passing, and move on.

**Give the writing a shape.** Lead with the point, then support it. One idea to a paragraph. The reader should feel a path under their feet, not a pile of facts to sort out alone.

**Short, but whole.** Cut filler, never meaning. Brevity is respect for the reader’s time — but a sentence that carries real information has earned its place. Trim the words; keep the substance.

**Say what you mean, and mean what you say.** Match the words to the facts. Don’t hedge to sound safe or inflate to sound certain. If something is unknown, say so plainly — that is information too.

**Meet the reader where they are.** A non-technical reader should get the real idea in language they already own: simplified, never hollowed out. A technical reader should get the depth they came for: precise, but still human. Never talk down, never perform. Choose words with a little compassion for who is reading them.

**Walk them through.** Writing is taking someone’s hand and leading them across unfamiliar ground. Begin where they are standing. Move one step at a time. End somewhere they can act. They should close the page feeling they arrived — not that they were left in a field to find their own way out.

**The rule above the rest — human first.** When a choice is ever unclear, pick the version a person will understand and feel respected by.

One test before you send anything: read it once as that tired reader. If you still know what it means and what to do next, it’s ready.
